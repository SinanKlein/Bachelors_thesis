
\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:overview}
This chapter describes how the grid of Section~\ref{data} is turned into a prediction problem, and what is
fitted on it. The same steps are applied to each of the three cohorts separately, since the cohorts do not
share a feature vocabulary.

Of the four matrices of Section~\ref{data}, $Y$ and $W$ supply the prediction targets $y$, $w_{\text{reg}}$ and
$w_{\text{class}}$, while $X_b$ and $X_v$ supply the features. Since the observation mask $M$ restricts $W$ to a
block of the full grid, we take all three targets on the pairs inside that block. This ensures that each model
is fitted and evaluated on the same pairs.

The protein features are not used in raw form. First, we reduce $X_b$ and $X_v$ to a common set of
protein clusters, then transform the remaining entries and finally combine the genus profile with the vOTU profile
into a single vector describing their pair. Each of the three steps is a choice rather than a necessity,
and we return to them for further details in Section~\ref{sec:features}.

Next, we divide the pairs into ten stratified folds. We fit each model on the training set and evaluate on the
held-out test set. The models we fit are a sparse logistic regression, a ridge regression, gradient-boosted
trees, a single-task neural network for each target, and a joint network that predicts $y$ and
$w_{\text{class}}$ from one shared representation. Since all of them are fitted on the same folds, they can be
compared fold by fold rather than only on average.

Two groups of analyses follow. The first asks whether the results depend on how the features were built, on
where the glasso values were thresholded, or on the taxonomic level at which the predictions are read. The
second asks what the shared representation of the joint model contributes. How the pipeline was configured and
run is described in Section~\ref{setup}.

\subsection{Task definitions}
\label{sec:tasks}
The three targets of Section~\ref{data} enter the pipeline as three separate prediction problems, in which a pair
is described by the same feature vector throughout. Two of them are binary classifications and one a continuous
regression. In $y$ we predict whether genus $i$ and vOTU $j$ are linked by a CRISPR spacer, and in
$w_{\text{class}}$ whether the glasso edge between them was selected in at least one subsample. In
$w_{\text{reg}}$ we predict the selection frequency $W_{ij}$ directly, so that the model is asked for the value
of the interaction and not only for whether it exceeds zero.

Since $W_{ij}$ is bounded between zero and one, nothing prevents the model from predicting a value outside that
range. Therefore, we clip the target away from the two endpoints, take its logit, and fit the regression on the
result. The predictions are transformed back to the original scale before any metric is computed, so the
reported $R^2$ refers to the original scale of $W$ and not to the logit scale on which the model was fitted.

\subsection{Feature representation}
\label{sec:features}

\subsubsection{Building the pair vector}
\label{sec:pairvector}
It is not feasible to pass raw protein-cluster profiles to the model since they are highly zero-inflated and
high dimensional. Thus, we reduce the two matrices before joining them since many entries carry zeroes with no
information. We do the reduction in three steps. Each step is a choice with two settings: which columns are
kept, what the kept entries are turned into, and how the genus profile and the vOTU profile are combined into a
single vector. None of the three is a necessity.

Selection ranks the columns of a matrix by their variance and keeps the highest
(Figures~\ref{fig:app-meanvar} and~\ref{fig:app-featcounts}). The score is always computed on
the raw counts and never on a transformed matrix, so the transform of the next step cannot change which clusters
are kept and the two steps stay independent of each other. We keep the two sides at equal width: each viral
cluster is kept and the bacterial matrix is cut to the same number by taking its $k$ columns of highest variance,
with $k$ the viral width. As Section~\ref{data} describes, $X_b$ spans an order of magnitude more protein
clusters than $X_v$ in every cohort, so the width of the pair vector is set by the viral side and most of the
bacterial side is discarded. How much of it survives is therefore not a quantity we set, but a consequence of
how many viral clusters a cohort contributes.

We then reduce the kept entries to presence/absence, so that an entry states whether the organism carries at
least one protein of that cluster and not how many it carries. The counts are discarded entirely.

Finally, we join the viral and bacterial profiles by adding them position by position, so that the pair vector
has the width of one side rather than of both. After the joining we have a vector with coordinates that are
either zero, one or two. This is possible only because selection made the widths equal. Bacteria and viruses
have separate protein-cluster vocabularies, which means that bacterial cluster $j$ and viral cluster $j$ are
unrelated to each other and their sum is not a statement about one specific protein family being present in
either partner. It is an architectural choice that halves the width of the pair vector and makes the model share
one weight between the two sides.

We did not fix these three settings in advance. Each was compared against its alternative before anything else
was fitted, and Section~\ref{sec:ablation} describes that comparison and the rule by which the settings used
here were selected. What is fitted on the resulting pair vectors is described in Section~\ref{sec:models}.

\subsubsection{Selecting the representation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Section~\ref{sec:pairvector} describes one representation, but each of its three choices could have been taken
differently, and a model reads a different pair vector under every combination of those settings. We call one
such combination an arm. Therefore, before committing to a single arm, we first performed an ablation experiment
to determine which arm performed best.

The first choice is the preprocessing step that determines which protein features are filtered. Equal-width
selection keeps each viral cluster and the same number of bacterial ones that explain the most variance. The
alternative keeps a fixed fraction of each of the two protein-feature matrices, the top 2.5\% of $X_b$ and the
top 10\% of $X_v$ by variance. This alternative leaves the bacterial half of the pair vector far wider than the
viral half. The comparison asks whether that imbalance matters, since a model given many more bacterial than
viral coordinates may favour the bacterial side for no reason other than its width.

The second choice is what the kept entries become. Presence/absence records only whether a cluster occurs, while
the centred log-ratio preserves how large a cluster is relative to the others in the same profile and is the
usual treatment of compositional count data. The comparison asks whether the abundance of a protein cluster
carries anything beyond its presence.

The third choice is how the profiles are joined. Summation adds them position by position whereas a
concatenation leaves each cluster its own coordinate and outputs a pair vector as wide as both sides together.
The comparison asks what is lost by folding the two halves onto one another. Since a vector sum requires the two
sides to have the same width, this choice is dependent on the first, and equal-width selection has to enter the
ladder before summation.

We compare the choices along a ladder of four arms rather than by analysing the full grid and also
measuring the interaction between them. The ladder, listed in Table~\ref{tab:arms}, begins from the arm in which
each choice takes the setting that was not adopted, and changes exactly one choice at each step until it arrives
at the one used in this thesis. Because only one choice moves at a time, the difference between two neighbouring
arms corresponds to the effect of that choice. During the ablation we use the same ten folds and the same
baseline models as the main experiment. We evaluate and report the ablation on the $y$ and $w_{\text{class}}$
tasks.

\subsection{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\subsubsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
As baselines we fit a sparse logistic regression to quantify the linearly learnable structure of the two
classification tasks, a ridge regression to quantify it for the regression task, and gradient-boosted trees as
well as a neural network to quantify the nonlinearly learnable structure of both. Gradient-boosted trees carry a
second role, since they are among the strongest general-purpose predictors on tabular data
\citep{chen2016,grinsztajn2022}, meaning they also serve as a demanding reference against which the neural
models are read.

Throughout, $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the pair vector of Section~\ref{sec:pairvector}, $t_i$ its label on the
task at hand and $n$ the number of pairs in the training partition of a fold. We fit every model except the
gradient-boosted trees on the standardised pair vector $\tilde{x}_i$, scale and standardize on the training partition
because a tree is unaffected by a monotone rescaling of a coordinate.

The sparse logistic regression models the log-odds of the label as a linear function of the pair vector,
penalised in $L_1$ norm,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:sparselogistic}
  \hat{\beta} = \arg\min_{\beta} \left\{ -C \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i \left[ t_i \log p_i + (1 - t_i) \log(1 - p_i)
  \right] + \|\beta\|_1 \right\} , \qquad p_i = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta^{\top} \tilde{x}_i) ,
\end{equation}
%
with $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$ the logistic function. The $L_1$ penalty sets most coefficients to exactly
zero, so the fitted model rests on a small number of coordinates instead of spreading weight over all of them.
The weights $c_i$ are inversely proportional to the frequency of the class of pair $i$, which keeps the rare
positives from being outweighed by the negatives.

For the regression task $w_{\text{reg}}$, we fit a ridge regression, where $t_i$ is the transformed target of
Section~\ref{sec:tasks}. Instead of cross-entropy and an $L_1$ penalty, we use squared error and an $L_2$
penalty,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ridge}
  \hat{\beta} = \arg\min_{\beta} \left\{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( t_i - \beta_0 - \beta^{\top} \tilde{x}_i \right)^{2}
  + \alpha \|\beta\|_2^{2} \right\} .
\end{equation}
%
Unlike the $L_1$ penalty, the $L_2$ penalty shrinks the coefficients towards zero without setting any of them
to it. This means that the fit keeps each of the coefficients and no selection takes place.

The gradient-boosted trees build an additive model out of regression trees, fitted one after another,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gbt}
  F_M(x) = F_0 + \nu \sum_{m=1}^{M} f_m(x) ,
\end{equation}
%
where each tree $f_m$ is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss at the current fit $F_{m-1}$ and scaled by
a learning rate $\nu$, with $F_0$ a constant initial fit and $M$ the number of trees. Since a tree splits on one
coordinate after another, the model can represent a response that depends on a combination of coordinates,
which a linear function of those same coordinates cannot.

The neural baseline is a network with a single hidden layer,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:mlp}
  h_i = \max\!\left(0,\; U_1 \tilde{x}_i + b_1\right) \in \mathbb{R}^{H} , \qquad
  \eta_i = u_2^{\top} \delta(h_i) + b_2 , \qquad p_i = \sigma(\eta_i) ,
\end{equation}
%
with $U_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times d}$ and $\delta$ dropping a random share of the hidden units during training
and acting as the identity at test time. On the classification tasks we fit the network on a class weighted
cross-entropy, in which the positive class is scaled by the inverse of its frequency in the training partition
and the negative class is left unweighted. We reweight the gradient-boosted trees in the same way. On the
regression task the output is $\eta_i$ itself and we fit on its squared error. For each task we fit a separate
network with the same structure and hyperparameters.

\subsubsection{Latent space models}
\label{sec:joint}
In order to test the claim of the thesis as stated in the introduction chapter, we now introduce latent space
models. In a latent space model, we first map the input features to a latent representation $h_i$ with a neural
network and then map that representation to the output with a single linear layer. The latent representation is
the $h_i$ of~(\ref{eq:mlp}), taken before dropout, so that it is exactly the vector the linear layer reads at
test time.

During the pipeline we fit three different latent space models. The first two are single task latent space models
where one is fitted on $y$ and one on $w_{\text{class}}$, such that each latent space model arranges its latent
space for one single task. The third latent space model is a joint model fitted for both targets simultaneously.
It keeps everything of~(\ref{eq:mlp}) before the output layer and replaces that single output by two, such that
one latent representation feeds a head for $y$ and a head for $w_{\text{class}}$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:joint}
  \eta_i^{y} = u_y^{\top} \delta(h_i) + b_y , \qquad \eta_i^{w} = u_w^{\top} \delta(h_i) + b_w ,
\end{equation}
%
with $u_y$ and $u_w$ the weight vectors of the two heads and $b_y$ and $b_w$ their biases. Writing $\ell$ for the
class-weighted cross-entropy of Section~\ref{sec:baselines} and $t_i^{y}$ and $t_i^{w}$ for the two labels of
pair $i$, we fit the network on the sum of the two losses,
%
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:jointloss}
  \mathcal{L} = \lambda_y \, \ell\!\left(\eta^{y}, t^{y}\right)
              + \lambda_w \, \ell\!\left(\eta^{w}, t^{w}\right) ,
\end{equation}
%
where we set $\lambda_y = \lambda_w = 1$, so that neither target is given priority over the other. Each head
carries its own class weights, since the two targets differ in prevalence.

Apart from the number of heads, the three latent space models are identical in structure and hyperparameters, so
a comparison between them isolates the effect of fitting one latent space for two targets rather than for one.
The regression task has no joint counterpart, since both heads are classifications.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:eval}
We evaluate each model by ten-fold cross-validation over the pairs, stratified on the $y$ label, so that each
pair lies in the test partition exactly once. Each model, task and ablation arm of a cohort uses the same ten
folds, which means any two models can be compared fold by fold rather than only on their averages. We stratify
on $y$ throughout, since one shared set of folds is what makes the tasks comparable and $y$ is the rarer of the
two binary targets.

The folds are drawn over pairs and not over organisms, so every genus and every vOTU appears in both partitions
and only their combination is new at test time. The evaluation is in this sense transductive and does not
describe how a model would behave on an organism it has never seen.

On the binary tasks, we report the AUC score together with the average precision, since at the positive rates
of Table~\ref{tab:descriptives} the first does not distinguish a good ranking overall from one whose top is
mostly wrong. On the regression task, we report $R^2$ on the original scale of $W$.

Since every model of a cohort sees the same ten folds, we compare two models by pairing their
per-fold test scores and reporting the $p$-value of a two-sided paired $t$-test over the ten
differences. These $p$-values are descriptive. Any two of the ten training partitions share most
of their pairs, so the differences are not independent, and there is no unbiased estimator of the
variance of a cross-validated score \citep{bengio2004}. The variance is therefore underestimated
and the test is anti-conservative. We also report many comparisons without correcting for their
number. We do not read a small $p$-value as evidence against a null hypothesis or as a proper tool
of statistical inference, but only as a difference that holds in fold after fold.

\subsection{Robustness analyses}
\label{sec:robustness}
Two further analyses ask whether the $w_{\text{class}}$ result reflects the target or the way we defined it. The
first varies where the continuous $W$ is cut, and the second varies the taxonomic level at which the predictions
are read. Either could have produced a target that is easier to predict than the one of
Section~\ref{sec:tasks}, and each holds everything else fixed, so that a difference can be attributed to the
choice that moved.

\subsubsection{Binarisation threshold}
\label{sec:wthreshold}
Section~\ref{sec:tasks} defines $w_{\text{class}}$ by thresholding $W$ at zero, which admits every pair selected
in at least one of the fifty subsamples. A stricter cut would define a different target and therefore may change the
prediction quality. In order to determine which cut yields a $w_{\text{class}}$ with a better prediction quality,
we repeat the classification task at ten evenly spaced thresholds inside $(0,1)$, refitting at each on the label
$\mathbf{1}[W_{ij} > t]$. At each threshold, we fit an $L_2$-penalised logistic regression ($C = 1.0$) and gradient-boosted
trees, so that the answer does not rest on one model. The reason why we fit the regression with an $L_2$ penalty
rather than an $L_1$ is that the $L_1$ is too slow on a CPU laptop and starts to become a bottleneck in this
task which would make the pipeline infeasible on it.

\subsubsection{Taxonomic resolution}
\label{sec:taxres}
Predictions are made at genus level, but a phage infecting one member of a bacterial family may infect others,
so the family may be the resolution at which the interaction is better defined. Therefore, we read the same
predictions at family level, without refitting any model, and aggregate to family-level predictions following
these steps: for each family, we take the median predicted probability over its member genera as the
family-level score, and label a family positive when the majority of its member genera carry a positive label.
We then aggregate within each fold, such
that a family-level score carries a standard deviation across folds. Since a median over several members varies
less than a single prediction does, aggregating may raise the measured performance whether or not the family
level carries information, which is worth keeping in mind when reading the comparison.

\subsection{The shuffled-Y control}
\label{sec:shuffley}
Section~\ref{sec:joint} fits one latent space for two targets, and if that helps $w_{\text{class}}$ there are two
reasons it might. Either $y$ carries structure that $w_{\text{class}}$ can use, or the second head merely
constrains the shared representation and the content of its labels does not matter. We separate the two alternatives
by refitting the joint model with the $y$ training labels permuted.

We apply the permutation inside the training partition only. The $w_{\text{class}}$ labels stay as they were
before, and each label the model is evaluated against stays real, such that the only thing that changes is what
the $y$ head is taught. We use the same folds, the same representation and the same seed as the joint model of
Section~\ref{sec:joint}, and repeat the permutation five times in each fold.

We state the reading in advance. If the real labels lift $w_{\text{class}}$ above the permuted ones, then $y$
carries structure that the shared representation passes to $w_{\text{class}}$. If the two are equal, the benefit
is the constraint of a second head and the content of its labels is irrelevant. The $y$ head itself is the check
that the permutation took effect, since a model taught permuted labels should not predict the real ones.
