On the $y$ task, arm 4 gives the highest, or near highest, mean AUC for both nonlinear models in
each cohort (CRC: MLP $0.812$, XGBoost $0.810$; GvHD: MLP $0.789$, XGBoost $0.782$; IBD: MLP
$0.810$, XGBoost $0.794$; Figure~\ref{fig:arm-ladder}). In CRC, this comes from a single step:
swapping the CLR-transformation for presence/absence (arm 1 $\to$ 2) gives an incremental advantage
to the nonlinear models (MLP $+0.023$ against a fold-gap SD of $0.012$; XGBoost $+0.034$ against
$0.025$) but not to sparse logistic ($+0.003$ against $0.009$), and adding equal-dimension selection
and summation (arm 2 $\to$ 4) moves both models by less than their own fold-gap SD, so neither
factor is decisive on its own. In GvHD, presence/absence only clears the bar for XGBoost ($+0.016$
against $0.015$); equal-dimension selection then drops both nonlinear models and sparse logistic
(MLP $-0.036$ against $0.033$; XGBoost $-0.029$ against $0.014$; sparse logistic $-0.015$ against
$0.010$), and summation recovers almost the same amount (MLP $+0.039$ against $0.031$; XGBoost
$+0.027$ against $0.012$; sparse logistic $+0.016$ against $0.008$), so arm 1 $\to$ 4 is not
decisive overall. In IBD, no step clears the bar for any model. Sparse logistic only moves opposite
to the nonlinear models in CRC, over the full ladder ($-0.011$ against $0.010$), driven mainly by
equal-dimension selection ($-0.020$ against $0.015$).

For CRC and IBD, on $w_{\text{class}}$, no arm is decisive. For GvHD however there are some decisive factors.
For example for the XGBoost model, arm 4 underperforms arm 1 ($-0.009$ against $0.006$). Besides XGBoost,
no other model is decisive either. Because of this reason, we cannot choose an arm solely based on the $w_{\text{class}}$
task result and need to rely on the $y$ task results. Additionally, we select the arm based on the MLP alone, since that is
the model the main experiment is built on via the latent space (Section~\ref{sec:joint}). XGBoost's agreement corroborates
the choice, without being decisive on its own. We keep the sparse logistic regression fixed as an unoptimised linear reference.

\subsection{Binarisation threshold}
\label{res:wthreshold}
As will be discussed in Section~\ref{res:main-wreg}, $w_{\text{reg}}$ performs poorly across all
cohorts, which is why we binarise it into $w_{\text{class}}$. As explained in
Section~\ref{sec:wthreshold}, we do this by fitting two different models across ten different
cutoffs, in order to determine which one is the most optimal.

  \label{fig:wthreshold}

No cohort or model exceeds $0.62$ AUC at any interior threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:wthreshold}). Additionally,
the variance of the AUC grows as the threshold rises. The reason is that with an increasing threshold, the number
of pairs that belong to the positive class shrinks: at $t=0$, $w_{\text{class}}$ is close to balanced (43\% positive
in CRC, 49\% in GvHD, 38\% in IBD) but by $t\approx0.5$ only 5--7\% of pairs remain positive, and the sweep excludes
$t=1$ itself, where $\mathbf{1}[W>t]$ would contain no positive pairs at all. That's consistent with $t=0$ being the
best cutoff among those tested for the $w_{\text{class}}$ task.


\subsection{Taxonomic resolution}
\label{res:taxres}
Genus-level AUC is slightly higher than family-level AUC across all three cohorts, on both tasks,
with only two trivial exceptions in GvHD (Figure~\ref{fig:family}; the underlying numbers are in
Table~\ref{tab:app-family}). Most families are singletons
(18 of 27 in CRC, 15 of 32 in GvHD, 20 of 34 in IBD), where genus and family level coincide by
construction and share the same label. The remaining, multi-member families do not always share a
consistent label across their genera, so the median aggregation used to collapse them discards real
heterogeneity, which causes the slight drop in AUC. On $y$, where this effect is real, sparse logistic
loses the most from it, since its per-genus probabilities carry the most direct signal, while the
nonlinear models lose comparatively little. On $w_{\text{class}}$, where no real effect exists to
begin with, no consistent pattern emerges across cohorts or models. We therefore base the resolution
choice on $y$ alone and fix genus level throughout the rest of the pipeline.

\subsection{Joint training and the shuffled-Y control}
\label{res:joint}
To test whether the joint model's benefit to $w_{\text{class}}$ (Section~\ref{res:main-wclass}) reflects
structure genuinely shared with $y$, or only the regularising effect of fitting a second head, we apply the
control of Section~\ref{sec:shuffley}: we retrain the joint MLP with the $y$ labels permuted within each
outer-fold training set, repeat this over five independent permutations, and evaluate both heads on the
unpermuted test fold. If the $w_{\text{class}}$ gain
survives even when $y$ carries no real signal, that gain cannot come from $y$ itself.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Shuffled-$y$ control: joint model performance, real vs permuted $y$ labels (differences: real $-$ permuted)}
  \label{tab:shuffle-y}
  \begin{tabular}{lllrr}
    \hline
    Cohort & Head               & $y$ labels & AUC   & AP    \\
    \hline
    CRC    & $y$                & Real       & 0.810 & 0.276 \\
           &                    & Permuted   & 0.485 & 0.042 \\
           & $w_{\text{class}}$ & Real       & 0.575 & 0.496 \\
           &                    & Permuted   & 0.579 & 0.497 \\
    \hline
    IBD    & $y$                & Real       & 0.802 & 0.271 \\
           &                    & Permuted   & 0.495 & 0.042 \\
           & $w_{\text{class}}$ & Real       & 0.608 & 0.475 \\
           &                    & Permuted   & 0.604 & 0.472 \\
    \hline
    GvHD   & $y$                & Real       & 0.789 & 0.191 \\
           &                    & Permuted   & 0.484 & 0.026 \\
           & $w_{\text{class}}$ & Real       & 0.601 & 0.587 \\
           &                    & Permuted   & 0.602 & 0.587 \\
    \hline
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The permutation removes the intended signal from the $y$ head: performance collapses to chance in every
cohort (AUC $0.484$--$0.495$ against $0.789$--$0.810$ with the real labels; AP $0.026$--$0.042$ against
$0.191$--$0.276$; Table~\ref{tab:shuffle-y}), and the drop is significant throughout ($p<10^{-7}$ on AUC
and $p\leq2\times10^{-4}$ on AP, all cohorts).

In the $w_{\text{class}}$ task, permuting $y$ leaves its performance unchanged in IBD (AUC $+0.004$, $p=0.377$; 
AP $+0.003$, $p=0.228$) and GvHD (AUC $-0.001$, $p=0.753$; AP $+0.000$, $p=0.994$). On the other hand, in the 
CRC cohort, it produces a small but significant change in the opposite direction (AUC $-0.005$, $p=0.022$; AP not 
significant, $p=0.828$). With real labels the model does very slightly \emph{worse} on
$w_{\text{class}}$ than with permuted ones. In no cohort does permuting $y$ significantly reduce
$w_{\text{class}}$ performance. The advantage the joint model holds over the single-task MLP in CRC and
GvHD (Section~\ref{res:main-wclass}) is reproduced in full by the permuted model, which reaches $0.579$
against $0.551$ in CRC and $0.602$ against $0.596$ in GvHD; in IBD there was no advantage to account for.
Since that advantage survives destroying $y$'s real
content entirely, it is not explained by structure shared between $y$ and $w_{\text{class}}$;
it is consistent with the plain regularising effect of fitting a second head, regardless of what that
head predicts.
